Dental advice

Practical answers to the questions we get asked most often at the practice.

How often should I have a check-up?

For most adults, every six months. If you're at higher risk of decay or gum disease, we may suggest more often; if your teeth and gums are consistently healthy, less often may be reasonable. We'll tell you what applies to you rather than applying a blanket rule.

How should I be brushing?

Twice a day, for two minutes, with a fluoride toothpaste and a soft brush. Angle the bristles slightly towards the gum line and use small movements rather than hard scrubbing — pressure damages gums without cleaning better. Spit out afterwards, but don't rinse; leaving the fluoride in contact with the teeth is the point.

Do I really need to floss?

Brushing misses the surfaces between teeth, which is exactly where decay and gum problems often start. Floss or interdental brushes reach those surfaces. Interdental brushes are easier for most people to use well, and if a tool is easier you're more likely to actually use it.

My gums bleed when I brush. Is that normal?

Common, but not normal. Bleeding gums usually indicate inflammation from plaque at the gum line. It often improves within a couple of weeks of consistent brushing and cleaning between the teeth. If it doesn't, book in — early gum disease is very treatable, and much easier to deal with early.

What counts as a dental emergency?

Severe or worsening pain, facial swelling, a knocked-out or badly broken tooth, or bleeding that won't stop. Call us on (07) 3366 6444 — we'll fit you in. If we're closed and it's urgent, see what to do after hours.

A tooth has been knocked out. What do I do?

Handle it by the crown, not the root. If it's an adult tooth and it's clean, try to put it straight back in the socket and bite gently on a cloth. If you can't, keep it in milk — not water — and get to a dentist immediately. Time matters a great deal here. Don't try to reinsert a baby tooth.

Is teeth grinding worth treating?

If it's wearing your teeth down or giving you jaw pain and morning headaches, yes. A custom night splint takes the load instead of your teeth. More about splints.

What about sugar and acid?

It's frequency more than quantity. Teeth recover between acid attacks, so constant grazing and sipping is worse than the same amount consumed at once. Sports drinks, fruit juice and soft drinks are acidic as well as sugary — water is the safest thing to sip through the day.

This page is general information, not personal advice. Your own situation may differ — ask us at your next appointment, or call the practice.
